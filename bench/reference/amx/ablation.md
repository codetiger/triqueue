## 2. Same model calibrated on Apple AMX (M2 Pro, measured)

## 3. The comparison that does not depend on precision

Express each engine's exposed drain in units of its own k step. Then utilization on a tile depends only on K: util ≈ K / (K + D/c_k).

| Engine | Cycles per k step | Exposed drain per tile | Drain in k steps | K at which utilization = 50% |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| AMX (1 accumulator, measured) | 4.29 | 68 | 16 | 16 |
| Tri-Queue, 1 bank (sim) | 1 | 19 | 19 | 19 |
| Tri-Queue, 2 banks (sim) | 1 | 0 once Kp ≥ 24 (1 cycle at Kp = 16) | 0 | – |

| K (64×64×K) | AMX measured | Tri-Queue 1 bank | Tri-Queue 2 banks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8 | 16.8% | 30.5% | 46.0% |
| 16 | 39.9% | 46.7% | 91.3% |
| 32 | 62.2% | 63.7% | 98.4% |
| 128 | 81.5% | 87.5% | 99.6% |
| 1024 | 90.9% | 98.2% | 99.9% |

**Reading:** measured in its own k steps, AMX's exposed drain (≈ 16) is about the same as a single-bank Tri-Queue's (19), and both lose about half their throughput at K ≈ 16–19. The same one-line model fits both within a few percent. Adding the second bank removes the loss in the simulator. The int8 vs fp32 difference doesn't enter any of these numbers. The remaining gap at large K (AMX 91% against the Tri-Queue's 98–100%) is AMX's ≈ 7% steady-state issue overhead (4.29 against 4 cycles per k step), which isn't about the accumulator.

## 4. Why AMX can't simply add a second bank in software

- Measured fma32 latency ≈ 4 ops, so full rate needs 4 independent accumulators.
- Z holds exactly 4 fp32 16×16 tiles (4 KB). A 32×32 tile uses all of them.
- Splitting Z into two 32×16 halves that take turns leaves 2 chains per half, which caps compute at about 50% (the 2-chain peak measurement: 50.5%).
- This limit comes from floating point: the accumulate latency forces 4 accumulators. The Tri-Queue's 1-cycle int accumulate needs only one per tile, which leaves room for a second bank. An fp32 Tri-Queue would face the same limit as AMX.
